## **What You’ll Learn**
This course is about more than *getting through calculus.* It’s about learning to **think like an engineer**. In engineering, problem-solving often moves through three roles:
- The **Physicist** builds the model, identifying the system and the governing principles.
- The **Mathematician** solves the model, using math tools to analyze how the system behaves.
- The **Engineer** uses the model, making design decisions and communicating results others can trust.

By the end of the semester, you will be able to:
- **Build Models (Physicist role):** Define systems and translate real-world situations into mathematical models.
- **Solve Models (Mathematician role):** Use calculus tools to analyze how outputs depend on inputs.
- **Use Models (Engineer role):** Interpret your solutions, make design decisions, and explain your reasoning in a way that others can trust.

Along the way, you’ll also strengthen essential engineering habits:
- **Collaboration:** Solve problems with peers the way engineering teams work in the field.
- **Communication:** Write up solutions clearly and professionally, using proper mathematical notation.
- **Confidence:** Tackle problems that seem intimidating at first, and leave each session surprised by how much stronger you’ve become.

## **Homework**

Homework in this course is done through _Knewton Alta_, an adaptive online platform. Unlike traditional problem sets, Alta adjusts to what you know and don’t know. If you’re struggling, it provides extra practice and targeted support. If you’ve mastered a concept, it moves you forward more quickly. The purpose of homework is to _build skill through practice_ - and Alta makes that practice efficient and personalized. 

Engineers thrive when they can recognize problem types, apply the right tools, and adapt quickly to new situations. That’s exactly what Alta trains you to do. The system doesn’t just mark answers right or wrong; it helps you identify where your process is breaking down and gives you the chance to fix it. By the time you face exams, you’ll have had the reps you need to solve problems confidently on your own.

### **How to Use Alta Well**

Alta is most powerful when you use it strategically. A few key habits make all the difference:

- **Don’t guess.** The system adapts to what you enter. Wrong answers caused by rushed guessing confuse the algorithm and make the road to mastery longer. Slow down, reason it out, and only submit an answer once you’ve genuinely worked through it.
- **Don’t cram.** Homework isn’t designed to be knocked out in one sitting. The brain builds deeper connections when you revisit material after some time away. Think of it like strength training: the “echo effect” of spacing out practice is where real gains happen.
- **Use the explanations.** When Alta provides a step-by-step explanation, treat it like having a tutor walk you through the problem. Pay attention not just to the steps, but to _why_ they work.
- **Work until it feels automatic.** The goal isn’t to finish the assignment with the least effort - it’s to develop a repeatable process you can use on exams and in engineering courses. That means solving until the steps feel natural.

### **Solution Write-Ups**

To complete each homework assignment, you will choose a problem to write up in professional presentation quality. These short assignments help you practice the skill of _writing like an engineer_. You’ll take a problem you’ve solved and explain it clearly, concisely, and in a way that a peer or supervisor could understand. You'll use proper math notation. We will give you feedback on these, and you will have unlimited opportunities to revise and resubmit. 

Why? Because in engineering, solving the problem is only half the job - communicating the solution is the other half. These write-ups are evaluated for clarity, reasoning, and professionalism, not just for the final answer. They help you develop one of the most valuable professional skills you’ll carry into your career.

## **Exams**

Exams are not just checkpoints - they are opportunities to measure what you can do _on your own_. Unlike homework, where you have time, resources, and tools to support your learning, exams recreate the conditions of real engineering practice when deadlines are tight and decisions must be made with only the information you’ve internalized.

This course is **cumulative by design.** Each exam builds on the foundations of earlier material, because mathematics itself is a layered system — later topics assume mastery of earlier ones. Preparing for an exam means not just reviewing the latest chapter, but weaving together everything you’ve learned so far.

Exams are taken **in person, on paper, and in pencil.** This format matters. Working by hand forces you to show your thinking step by step, a skill just as critical as arriving at the final answer. It also removes the crutches of digital tools so that what’s left is your own reasoning. By practicing with homework and write-ups in advance, you’ll be ready to demonstrate mastery under these conditions.

Exams are offered in-person during your Discussion session. If you’re registered with the DRC, please reach out so that we can schedule your exam through their testing center (you will be excused from Discussion that week).

## **Time Commitment**

UNLV expects students to spend **2–3 hours per week outside of class for every credit hour**. Since this is a 4-credit course, that translates to **8–12 hours of homework and study per week**. I design the course with that expectation in mind. Some weeks will feel heavy and others lighter, but your best strategy is to dedicate a steady 8–12 hours every week. When a week is light, use the extra time to get ahead, revisit past assignments, or strengthen areas where you struggled. When a week is heavier, know that the load balances out over time.

Think of it this way: if you always budget and invest those hours, you’ll stay on top of the material and avoid being caught off guard. If you ever find the work consistently takes more than 12 hours, let me know - that’s feedback I take seriously. And if you find yourself finishing early, that’s your opportunity to **go deeper**: re-work problems without tools, create your own variations, or challenge yourself to explain concepts out loud as if you were teaching them.

Ultimately, the goal isn’t to “check the boxes” but to **train your brain** in problem-solving habits that will serve you far beyond this semester. Think of these hours as an **investment in your future self as an engineer**.

## **Support & Resources**

One of the biggest shifts from high school to college is how we think about support. In K–12 education, needing tutoring often carried a stigma - as if it meant you were “behind” or “deficient.” And “going to the office” usually meant you were in trouble. Here, it’s the opposite. In college, using the resources around you is not a sign of weakness - it’s a sign of strength. You are paying for access to an incredible program, filled with opportunities to accelerate your learning. Why try to push through alone when the tools to save you time and effort are already built into the system?

Think of tutoring, office hours, review sessions, and study groups as **shortcuts** to mastery. My mantra is: _15 minutes with a professor or tutor can often replace 4 hours of solo work._ Engineering is demanding, and brute-force studying is not sustainable. The smartest learners - the ones who get the most out of college - are the ones who know when to reach for help and how to use it strategically.

> You won’t impress me by saying *“I stayed up all night and I finally figured it out!”* You will impress me by saying *“I started early, got stuck, went to office hours or tutoring, and figured it out!”* Learning efficiently is more important than doing it all on your own.’
