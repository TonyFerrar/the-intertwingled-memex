# Session 2 - Area Under a Curve and Definite Integrals

## Session Overview
For engineers, the "area under a curve" is far more than a geometric abstraction; it is the fundamental tool for calculating **accumulation** in dynamic systems. While the derivative tells you the instantaneous rate of change—such as the flow rate of water through a pipe or the power consumption of a circuit—the **definite integral**, denoted as:

$$\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$$

allows you to calculate the *total* quantity accumulated over an interval (e.g., total volume or total energy). In practical terms, whenever you are dealing with **variable forces**, **changing densities**, or **non-uniform rates** where simple multiplication ($\text{rate} \times \text{time}$) fails, integration provides the precise total impact necessary for design and analysis.

>These handouts explain how to determine the **[[Net Area]]** located between a mathematical function and the horizontal axis. While basic shapes can be solved using **geometric formulas**, more complex curves require the use of **definite integrals** to find precise values. A critical concept presented is that regions sitting **below the x-axis** are treated as negative values, which can result in a total net area of zero or less. These notes also establish the **[[The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)|Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]]**, proving that these areas can be calculated by finding the **antiderivative** of a function and evaluating the difference between two endpoints. By utilizing the **Mean Value Theorem**, the materials demonstrate how complex summations of small rectangles transition into streamlined calculus operations. Various **mathematical properties** and worked examples, ranging from linear equations to trigonometric curves, illustrate these core integration techniques.
