# Here's what I said

## Making the Most of Your Experience

### Opening
I delivered my first research presentation at an aerospace engineering conference in Denver, 2009. I was one year into my masters and had obtained the first results from my jet engine experiment. I had recently made the decision to pursue my PhD and dedicate my career to academia as an engineering professor. I felt tremendous pressure to impress: this was my first chance to make myself known to my future colleagues.

Looking back, I know that I over-prepared by essentially performing the entire lit review for my dissertation. 20 minutes into my 25-minute presentation, the only thing I’d spoken about was the history of the problem I was researching and what others had done to try and solve it. The kicker was that, as with most academic research, my work was niche enough that my audience was mostly comprised of the authors of the papers I referenced (unbeknownst to me). I gave a 20-minute lecture to highly-experienced professionals about their own work!

I learned a few valuable lessons that day: First, know your audience and what they are looking for. Second, speak about your expertise rather than the work of others aside from providing context. Third, we don’t have to start at the beginning and travel through all of history every time we talk about our work.

I’m afraid that I’ll violate at least two of these rules today. I celebrated my 40th birthday in October 2024, and I find myself enamored with big-picture thinking, searching for the meaning of life and all that. Now it is your misfortune to have to see where I’m at in that particular journey.

>*“Suddenly, the Universe lit up with billions of hot spots, each pumping energy into the cold of surrounding space.”* - David Christian, Big History

### Point 1: Be Here
The universe began about 13 billion years ago. For someone who measures a lifetime in just a few decades, this is an incomprehensible timescale. To see the major milestones on our journey from the Big Bang to today, let’s follow Professor Christian’s approach and imagine it as a 13 (rather than 13 billion) year timeline.

The history of the Universe is a long journey from diffuse simplicity to concentrated complexity.

What’s the point? Why this history lesson? To remind us that (1) we and our problems are small, and (2) that small things can be very important. It may be self-congratulatory to say it, but from this perspective of ever-increasing complexity, humans and human civilization represent an end to which all of the Universe has been laboring for 13 billion years (at least so far).

What was on your mind before you traveled here? What are you worrying about that could keep you from fully-engaging in this experience?

I bet I can guess some of it. Debugging an experiment, performing an analysis. Meeting a publication deadline. Tracking down that perfect reference. Passing a class while trying to write a dissertation. Standing in front of a classroom trying to project confidence while suffering from impostor syndrome on the inside. Paying a bill, fixing your busted car on a grad student’s income. Deciding between a post doc or an industry job and whether you can take another few years of sacrifice before the promise of a real salary finally comes true. Uncertainty in higher education as a whole given the state of political affairs.

>*“How do I know what is going to turn up? My business is to use what does turn up with diligence and skill… it is for you, then to take what is given you and make the most of it.”* - Epictetus 

My first point, and I know I took a long time getting here, is to recognize that this is a unique moment in the story of your life. You’ll never get to come back here and do this again. Can you be here? Can you make the most of this moment that’s been given to you? Can you put down the other things you’re worried about, knowing they will be fine waiting until you get to back to them in a few days?

### Point 2: Connect Ideas
Continuing on our journey through history and the transition from diffuse simplicity to concentrated complexity, let’s look at humanity for a moment. For most of our history, about 250,000 years, we wandered around in small groups of about 30 people. Imagine that. There are more people in this room than most humans encountered in their entire lives. For most of human history, things were pretty simple and progress (at least how it is measured today in terms of technology and wealth) was pretty slow.

When we stopped wandering about 5,000 years ago, things changed rapidly. Written language appeared: first as numbers to tally crop yields in our burgeoning agrarian societies and then as symbolic language capable of capturing abstract ideas.

What is it that makes us human? Collective learning. So to be self-congratulatory again, your interest in academics is an interest in a career doing the most human thing we can do: investing in collective learning. We are the ones to promulgate the lessons of the past and generate the knowledge of the future.

Something I learned here at BFF was to always be reading a book unrelated to my discipline. I took that advice and started a goal of one nonfiction book per month. 9 years later I’ve read over 100 books ranging from economics to marketing to stoic philosophy to the history of the universe and humanity. As I read, I try to connect what I am learning to my own work. For example, I use marketing techniques in the classroom alongside actual pedagogy.

My second point, also arrived at via a winding path, is to take notes, and share them with each other. The notes I took during my own BFF experience may as well be my user manual to a successful faculty career. I still refer to it regularly, 9 years later! In fact, it’s the reason I am here. Every time I found something useful, I’d track down the speaker and drop them a “thank you” email. This is what led to me being invited back once, and the rest is history. Find ways to connect what you’re learning, even when it seems unrelated.

### Point 3: Connect to Each Other
I’d like to circle back to the early universe. What was going on just after the Big Bang?

>*“Gravity can magnify even the tiniest unevenness, because its power increases where more matter is concentrated. Where matter and energy are not distributed evenly, gravity can rearrange them in more interesting ways.*
>
>*The early universe contained vast clouds of hydrogen and helium… with tiny differences in density and energy. Gravity magnified these differences, splitting vast clouds of matter into billions of smaller clouds.*
>
>*As each cloud contracted under the action of gravity, atoms collided with increasing frequency and violence. Eventually, in a sudden phase change, the violence of these collisions overcame the forces driving them apart.*
>
>*Suddenly, the Universe lit up with billions of hot spots, each pumping energy into the cold of surrounding space.”* - David Christian, Big History

I see this as an image of hope for what’s happening in the world right now. Just as the universe has been moving from diffuse simplicity to concentrated complexity, so has humanity. The frequency and violence of our collisions seem to be increasing. Perhaps, like that fight and subsequent making up that actually solidified your closest relationship, humanity is nearing a sort of critical mass and we’re about to overcome the forces driving us apart. I believe it is our jobs as academics to do this.

There are places in the space-time of our lives where we experience a sort of gravitational effect. For me, this place is one of them. When I was in your position here, all I was really thinking about was getting a job that involved making loud things go faster. What I found instead would become the higher purpose for my career. 

They may not be allowed to say this, but I am a guest speaker which gives me some reduced accountability. This program was created and offered by the NC State Office of Institutional Equity and Diversity. The application screened participants to find those committed to making impact as academics on the frontlines of DEI. I didn’t know why I’d been invited, not only because I look like “the bad guy” but also because I hadn’t really taken the time to learn about my privilege or the experiences of people who don’t have it. I spent those three days learning an awful lot, and the entire direction of my career changed. Colliding with those people ignited something within me, and it is now my full time job to build success among underrepresented students at UNLV.

I am so very grateful to those scholars who shared their experiences with me and helped me learn.

And so my third, and final point: Talk to each other, every chance you get. Skip the small talk, the posturing. Share your real experience, learn about each others’. Let the gravity of this place do its work of magnifying even the tiniest inequality because as we come together, our concentrated power increases.

Grad school and early years of faculty life are wildly different. Grad students tend to bond as a sort of cohort, you have buddies and you take turns supporting each other. Being a professor can be lonely even though you’re always around people. It’s not that folks are less friendly, it’s just they’re all busy with their responsibilities and very few of them involve collaboration. Yet, you’ll need external collaborations and reviewers to earn things like promotion and tenure. This experience is special, you won’t have many opportunities to connect across disciplines and colleges. I wish I had kept up better with my own BFF cohort.

### Closing Inspiration
I have the problem of thinking in paragraphs and pages instead of sentences. I fear I’ve done that here in an attempt to give a talk very different from my usual lectures on calculus for engineers. What I hope you take away from this is to:
1. Be here, be present with us. 
2. Connect ideas, taking notes on everything even if you think it might not apply to you. 
3. Connect to each other, sharing your genuine selves in a rare chance to collaborate with noncompetitors. 

I know what you’re worried about and the practical concerns you’re hoping to address here. I was worried about the same things when I came: chalk talk and research proposals, landing grants, advising your first grad student. Managing grants, reporting. Writing articles, building a research program, earning tenure before you lose your job. Teaching classes, standing in front of students and combating that pernicious imposter syndrome. You’re going to learn to deal with all of that here if you pay attention.

But don’t miss this rare chance to take a deeper look at your work and your motives. What difference does it really make if your technology is created? Your idea spread? What’s the point? To paraphrase the Stoic philosophers: to increase our ability to use reason to live socially - to create the best society that is humanly possible. 

Maybe we’re at the brink of a global, sudden phase change. Maybe we’ve finally reached the point at which the frequency and violence of our collisions will be able to overcome the forces which drive us apart. Maybe humanity is traveling toward a bright future. I think the answer to that question depends on us and what we do next.

>*“Suddenly, the universe lit up with billions of hot spots, each pumping energy and light into the cold of surrounding space.”* - David Christian, Big History

Thank you!
