# Skill 1: Center of Mass in Two Dimensions (2D)

## 1. Topic Overview & Core Notes

### What is the Center of Mass?

The **center of mass** (often called the center of gravity in a uniform gravitational field) represents the unique balance point of an object—the point where the entire mass of the plate can be considered to be concentrated.

- **Lamina:** A flat, two-dimensional plate with boundaries defined in the $xy$-plane.
    
- **Uniform vs. Variable Density:**
    
    - If the density $\rho(x,y)$ is **constant**, the mass is distributed evenly. The balance point depends purely on geometry and is called the **centroid**.
        
    - If the density $\rho(x,y)$ **varies** across the plate (e.g., due to uneven cooling, composite materials, or internal components), the center of mass shifts toward denser regions.
        

### Essential Formulas

#### 1. Total Mass ($M$)

Accumulates density across the area of the region $R$:

$$M = \iint_R \rho(x,y) \, dA$$

#### 2. First Moments ($M_y$ and $M_x$)

First moments measure the cumulative structural leverage (mass times distance) relative to the coordinate axes:

- **Moment about the y-axis ($M_y$):** Measures horizontal leverage relative to $x = 0$:
    
    $$M_y = \iint_R x \, \rho(x,y) \, dA$$
    
- **Moment about the x-axis ($M_x$):** Measures vertical leverage relative to $y = 0$:
    
    $$M_x = \iint_R y \, \rho(x,y) \, dA$$
    

#### 3. Center of Mass Coordinates $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$

Dividing the directional leverage by total mass scales the result to give spatial coordinates:

$$\overline{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}, \quad \overline{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}$$

### Choosing the Right Coordinate System

|**Feature**|**Rectangular Coordinates**|**Polar Coordinates**|
|---|---|---|
|**Area Element ($dA$)**|$dA = dy \, dx$ or $dx \, dy$|$dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$|
|**Best Used For**|Straight-line boundaries (rectangles, triangles, parabolas)|Circular, semi-circular, or wedge-shaped regions|
|**Key Substitutions**|Direct $x$ and $y$ variables|$x = r \cos\theta, \quad y = r \sin\theta$|

> **Critical Jacobian Reminder:** When converting to polar coordinates, never forget the extra **$r$** factor in $dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$!

### Step-by-Step Solution Roadmap

1. **Sketch & Define Region $R$:** Identify the bounding equations and intersection points.
    
2. **Assign Density $\rho(x,y)$:** Use $\rho = \text{constant}$ for uniform plates or the given function for non-uniform plates.
    
3. **Check for Symmetry:** If the geometry and density are symmetric across an axis, the center of mass coordinate along that axis lies directly on the symmetry line.
    
4. **Set Up & Evaluate Integrals:** Compute $M$, $M_y$, and $M_x$.
    
5. **Calculate $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$:** Divide $M_y / M$ and $M_x / M$.
    

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Axis/Variable Swapping:** Remember that $M_y$ uses the horizontal distance $x$, while $M_x$ uses the vertical distance $y$.
    
- **Confusing Centroid with Center of Mass:** Centroid is purely geometric ($\rho = \text{constant}$), whereas center of mass includes variable density.
    
- **Forgetting Boundary Dependencies:** In Type I regions, inner bounds depend on $x$ (e.g., $y = 0$ to $y = g(x)$); outer bounds must always be constants.
    

## 2. Standard Mathematical Practice Problems

### Problem 1: Rectangular Plate with Variable Linear Density Gradient

A thin rectangular lamina occupies the region bounded by $0 \le x \le 2$ and $0 \le y \le 1$. Its mass density varies according to:

$$\rho(x,y) = x + 2y$$

Find the total mass $M$ and the center of mass coordinates $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$.

#### Solution:

**Step 1: Compute Total Mass ($M$)**

$$M = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{1} (x + 2y) \, dy \, dx$$

Inner integral with respect to $y$:

$$\int_{0}^{1} (x + 2y) \, dy = \left[ xy + y^2 \right]_0^1 = x + 1$$

Outer integral with respect to $x$:

$$M = \int_{0}^{2} (x + 1) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} + x \right]_0^2 = \left(\frac{4}{2} + 2\right) - 0 = 4$$

**Step 2: Compute $M_y$ (Moment about y-axis)**

$$M_y = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{1} x(x + 2y) \, dy \, dx = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{1} (x^2 + 2xy) \, dy \, dx$$

Inner integral:

$$\int_{0}^{1} (x^2 + 2xy) \, dy = \left[ x^2 y + xy^2 \right]_0^1 = x^2 + x$$

Outer integral:

$$M_y = \int_{0}^{2} (x^2 + x) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^2 = \frac{8}{3} + 2 = \frac{14}{3}$$

**Step 3: Compute $M_x$ (Moment about x-axis)**

$$M_x = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{1} y(x + 2y) \, dy \, dx = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{1} (xy + 2y^2) \, dy \, dx$$

Inner integral:

$$\int_{0}^{1} (xy + 2y^2) \, dy = \left[ \frac{xy^2}{2} + \frac{2y^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{x}{2} + \frac{2}{3}$$

Outer integral:

$$M_x = \int_{0}^{2} \left(\frac{x}{2} + \frac{2}{3}\right) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{2x}{3} \right]_0^2 = 1 + \frac{4}{3} = \frac{7}{3}$$

**Step 4: Compute Coordinates $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$**

$$\overline{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{14/3}{4} = \frac{7}{6} \approx 1.17$$

$$\overline{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{7/3}{4} = \frac{7}{12} \approx 0.58$$

$$\mathbf{(\overline{x}, \overline{y}) = \left(\frac{7}{6}, \frac{7}{12}\right) \approx (1.17, 0.58)}$$

### Problem 2: Polar Quarter-Disk with Radial Density

A component shaped like a quarter-disk of radius $R = 2$ lies in the first quadrant ($0 \le r \le 2, \, 0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$). Its density increases linearly with distance from the origin:

$$\rho(r, \theta) = r$$

Find the center of mass coordinates $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$.

#### Solution:

**Step 1: Compute Total Mass ($M$)**

$$M = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \int_{0}^{2} (r) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} d\theta \int_{0}^{2} r^2 \, dr$$

$$M = \left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) \left[ \frac{r^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{8}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3}$$

**Step 2: Compute $M_y$ (using $x = r\cos\theta$)**

$$M_y = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \int_{0}^{2} (r \cos\theta) \cdot (r) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \left( \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \, d\theta \right) \left( \int_{0}^{2} r^3 \, dr \right)$$

$$M_y = [\sin\theta]_0^{\pi/2} \cdot \left[ \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^2 = (1) \cdot (4) = 4$$

**Step 3: Compute $M_x$ (using $y = r\sin\theta$)**

$$M_x = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \int_{0}^{2} (r \sin\theta) \cdot (r) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta = \left( \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin\phi \, d\phi \right) \left( \int_{0}^{2} r^3 \, dr \right)$$

$$M_x = [-\cos\theta]_0^{\pi/2} \cdot (4) = (1) \cdot (4) = 4$$

**Step 4: Compute Coordinates $(\overline{x}, \overline{y})$**

$$\overline{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{4}{4\pi/3} = \frac{3}{\pi} \approx 0.955$$

$$\overline{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{4}{4\pi/3} = \frac{3}{\pi} \approx 0.955$$

$$\mathbf{(\overline{x}, \overline{y}) = \left(\frac{3}{\pi}, \frac{3}{\pi}\right) \approx (0.955, 0.955)}$$
