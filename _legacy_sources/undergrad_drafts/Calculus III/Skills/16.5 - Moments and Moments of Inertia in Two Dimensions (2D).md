# Skill 2: Moments and Moments of Inertia in Two Dimensions (2D)

## 1. Topic Overview & Core Notes

### What are Moments of Inertia?

While first moments ($M_x, M_y$) measure **linear leverage** to locate the balance point (center of mass), **second moments of inertia** ($I_x, I_y, I_z$) measure **rotational leverage**. They quantify how resistant a 2D structure or component is to bending or rotational acceleration around a specified axis.

- **Mass Distribution:** The further material is placed from an axis, the harder it is to rotate or flex around that axis. Because distance is **squared** in second moments, doubling the distance from the axis quadruples the rotational resistance.
    
- **Flexural Rigidity:** In structural design, a higher moment of inertia means greater resistance to sagging or bending under heavy loads.
    

### Essential Formulas

#### 1. Second Moment about the x-axis ($I_x$)

Measures resistance to bending/rotation around the horizontal $x$-axis (uses vertical distance squared, $y^2$):

$$I_x = \iint_R y^2 \, \rho(x,y) \, dA$$

#### 2. Second Moment about the y-axis ($I_y$)

Measures resistance to bending/rotation around the vertical $y$-axis (uses horizontal distance squared, $x^2$):

$$I_y = \iint_R x^2 \, \rho(x,y) \, dA$$

#### 3. Polar Moment of Inertia ($I_z$)

Measures resistance to twisting/rotation in the $xy$-plane around the perpendicular $z$-axis (passing through the origin):

$$I_z = \iint_R (x^2 + y^2) \, \rho(x,y) \, dA = I_x + I_y$$

#### 4. Radius of Gyration ($k$)

The **radius of gyration** ($k$) represents the effective distance from an axis at which the plate's entire total mass $M$ could be concentrated into a single point to yield the exact same moment of inertia ($I = M k^2$):

$$k_x = \sqrt{\frac{I_x}{M}}, \quad k_y = \sqrt{\frac{I_y}{M}}, \quad k_z = \sqrt{\frac{I_z}{M}}$$

### Step-by-Step Solution Roadmap

1. **Identify the Axis of Rotation:** Determine whether you need $I_x$ (around the x-axis), $I_y$ (around the y-axis), or $I_z$ (around the origin).
    
2. **Compute Total Mass ($M$):** Required whenever calculating a radius of gyration $k$.
    
3. **Select Coordinates:** Use rectangular coordinates ($dA = dy \, dx$) for straight boundaries or polar coordinates ($dA = r \, dr \, d\theta$) for circular arcs/rings.
    
4. **Set Up & Integrate:** Include the squared distance term ($y^2$, $x^2$, or $r^2$) alongside the density $\rho(x,y)$.
    
5. **Calculate Radius of Gyration ($k$):** Take the square root of the moment divided by total mass ($k = \sqrt{I/M}$).
    

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Swapping Variable Terms:** A common mistake is using $x^2$ for $I_x$; remember that $I_x$ measures vertical distance from the x-axis, so it requires $y^2$.
    
- **Confusing First and Second Moments:** $M_x$ and $M_y$ use linear distances ($y$ and $x$) for center of mass; $I_x$ and $I_y$ use squared distances ($y^2$ and $x^2$) for rotational inertia.
    
- **Omitting the Mass Calculation:** You cannot compute the radius of gyration $k$ without first solving for total mass $M$.
    

## 2. Standard Mathematical Practice Problems

### Problem 1: Rectangular Plate with Uniform Density

A rectangular plate occupies $0 \le x \le 2$ and $0 \le y \le 3$ with uniform density $\rho = 2$. Compute $M$, $I_x$, $I_y$, $I_z$, and the radius of gyration $k_z$ about the origin.

#### Solution:

**Step 1: Compute Total Mass ($M$)**

$$M = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{3} 2 \, dy \, dx = 2 \int_{0}^{2} [y]_0^3 \, dx = 2 \int_{0}^{2} 3 \, dx = 12$$

**Step 2: Compute Moment of Inertia about x-axis ($I_x$)**

$$I_x = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{3} y^2 (2) \, dy \, dx = 2 \int_{0}^{2} \left[ \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^3 \, dx = 2 \int_{0}^{2} 9 \, dx = 36$$

**Step 3: Compute Moment of Inertia about y-axis ($I_y$)**

$$I_y = \int_{0}^{2} \int_{0}^{3} x^2 (2) \, dy \, dx = 2 \int_{0}^{3} dy \int_{0}^{2} x^2 \, dx = 2(3) \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = 6 \left(\frac{8}{3}\right) = 16$$

**Step 4: Compute Polar Moment of Inertia ($I_z$) & Radius of Gyration ($k_z$)**

$$I_z = I_x + I_y = 36 + 16 = 52$$

$$k_z = \sqrt{\frac{I_z}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{52}{12}} = \sqrt{\frac{13}{3}} \approx 2.08$$

$$\mathbf{M = 12, \quad I_x = 36, \quad I_y = 16, \quad I_z = 52, \quad k_z = \sqrt{\frac{13}{3}} \approx 2.08}$$

### Problem 2: Triangular Plate with Variable Boundary

A triangular lamina occupies the region bounded by $y = 0$, $x = 3$, and $y = x$ with a uniform density $\rho = 1$. Compute $I_x$, $I_y$, $I_z$, and $k_z$.

#### Solution:

**Step 1: Compute Total Mass ($M$)**

$$M = \int_{0}^{3} \int_{0}^{x} 1 \, dy \, dx = \int_{0}^{3} x \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^3 = \frac{9}{2} = 4.5$$

**Step 2: Compute $I_x$**

$$I_x = \int_{0}^{3} \int_{0}^{x} y^2 \, dy \, dx = \int_{0}^{3} \left[ \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_0^x \, dx = \int_{0}^{3} \frac{x^3}{3} \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^4}{12} \right]_0^3 = \frac{81}{12} = \frac{27}{4} = 6.75$$

**Step 3: Compute $I_y$**

$$I_y = \int_{0}^{3} \int_{0}^{x} x^2 \, dy \, dx = \int_{0}^{3} x^2 [y]_0^x \, dx = \int_{0}^{3} x^3 \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^3 = \frac{81}{4} = 20.25$$

**Step 4: Compute $I_z$ and $k_z$**

$$I_z = I_x + I_y = 6.75 + 20.25 = 27$$

$$k_z = \sqrt{\frac{I_z}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{27}{4.5}} = \sqrt{6} \approx 2.45$$

$$\mathbf{I_x = 6.75, \quad I_y = 20.25, \quad I_z = 27, \quad k_z = \sqrt{6} \approx 2.45}$$
