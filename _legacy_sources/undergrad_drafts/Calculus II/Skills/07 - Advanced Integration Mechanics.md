# Skill 3: Advanced Integration Mechanics (Substitution Focus)

Setting up a volume integral is only half the battle. Once your shell components are properly formulated using the core cylindrical shell theorems, you must execute the calculus. Because the shell method inherently multiplies a radius variable ($x$ or $y$) by a height expression, the resulting integrands frequently require specialized integration techniques centered around $u$-substitution and advanced exponent algebra.

## Explanatory Notes: Mastering the Substitution Methods

When evaluating a definite integral generated by the shell method without relying on integration by parts, you will typically depend on two main variation techniques of $u$-substitution:

### 1. Standard $u$-Substitution (The Radius as a Derivative Helper)

Many shell method problems feature highly complex internal composite functions. Normally, functions containing terms like $x^2$ inside a radical or exponential function are difficult to integrate on their own. However, because the shell method places a standalone variable ($x$) outside the function as the radius, it serves as the perfect "derivative helper" to complete a standard $u$-substitution.

### 2. Algebraic Back-Substitution (The "Change of Variables" Twist)

When the derivative of your chosen $u$ does not perfectly eliminate the remaining outer variables, you must use algebraic back-substitution. In this method, you explicitly solve your substitution equation for the original variable (e.g., rewriting $u = x - 1$ as $x = u + 1$) and substitute that expression back into the remaining outer terms of the integrand.

Whenever you apply either substitution method to a definite integral, always remember to convert your limits of integration to match your new variable $u$ before integrating.

## Sample Problems

### Sample Problem 1: Algebraic Back-Substitution ($u$-Substitution Twist)

**Problem:** Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x-1}$, the $x$-axis, and the vertical line $x = 5$ about the $y$-axis.

**Solution:**

1. **Setup:** Slicing parallel to the vertical $y$-axis gives a vertical shell layout ($dx$) evaluated over the interval from $x = 1$ to $x = 5$.
    
    - $\text{Radius } r(x) = x$
        
    - $\text{Height } h(x) = \sqrt{x-1} - 0 = \sqrt{x-1}$
        
        $$V = 2\pi \int_{1}^{5} x\sqrt{x-1} \, dx$$
        
2. **Algebraic Back-Substitution:** Let $u = x - 1 \implies du = dx$.
    
    - Notice that the outer radius variable $x$ does not naturally cancel out. We must solve for $x$ in terms of $u$:
        
        $$x = u + 1$$
        
    - Convert the integration limits to match $u$:
        
        - Lower limit: When $x = 1$, $u = 1 - 1 = 0$.
            
        - Upper limit: When $x = 5$, $u = 5 - 1 = 4$.
            
3. **Substitute and Distribute:** Replace all variables with $u$ terms and apply fractional exponents ($\sqrt{u} = u^{1/2}$):
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{4} (u + 1)u^{1/2} \, du$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{4} (u^{3/2} + u^{1/2}) \, du$$
    
4. **Integrate and Compute:** Apply the standard power rule to each term:
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{2}{5}u^{5/2} + \frac{2}{3}u^{3/2} \right]_{0}^{4}$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \left[ \frac{2}{5}(4)^{5/2} + \frac{2}{3}(4)^{3/2} \right] - 0 \right)$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{2}{5}(32) + \frac{2}{3}(8) \right) = 2\pi \left( \frac{64}{5} + \frac{16}{3} \right)$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{192 + 80}{15} \right) = \frac{544\pi}{15}$$
    

### Sample Problem 2: Standard $u$-Substitution (Rational Radical Integrand)

**Problem:** The region between the curve $y = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$ and the $x$-axis on the interval $[0,3]$ is rotated about the $y$-axis. Use the cylindrical shell method to find the exact volume of the generated solid.

**Solution:**

1. **Setup:** Slicing parallel to the vertical $y$-axis gives a standard $dx$ configuration over the interval $[0,3]$.
    
    - $\text{Radius } r(x) = x$
        
    - $\text{Height } h(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$
        
        $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{3} x \cdot \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}\right) \, dx = 2\pi \int_{0}^{3} \frac{x^2}{\sqrt{x^3+1}} \, dx$$
        
2. **Standard $u$-Substitution:** Let $u = x^3 + 1$. The derivative is $du = 3x^2 \, dx$, which means our outer $x^2$ term serves as the ideal derivative helper:
    
    $$x^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{3} \, du$$
    
    - Convert the integration limits to match $u$:
        
        - Lower limit: When $x = 0$, $u = 0^3 + 1 = 1$.
            
        - Upper limit: When $x = 3$, $u = 3^3 + 1 = 28$.
            
3. **Evaluate the Integral:**
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{1}^{28} \frac{1}{\sqrt{u}} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{3} \, du\right) = \frac{2\pi}{3} \int_{1}^{28} u^{-1/2} \, du$$
    
    $$V = \frac{2\pi}{3} \Big[ 2u^{1/2} \Big]_{1}^{28} = \frac{4\pi}{3} \Big[ \sqrt{u} \Big]_{1}^{28}$$
    
    $$V = \frac{4\pi}{3} (\sqrt{28} - \sqrt{1}) = \frac{4\pi}{3}(2\sqrt{7} - 1) = \frac{8\pi\sqrt{7} - 4\pi}{3}$$
