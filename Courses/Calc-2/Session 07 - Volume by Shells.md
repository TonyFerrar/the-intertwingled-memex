### Micro-Lecture
#### Engineering Context: Engineering Calculus II Application
Mastering geometric visualization, component formulation, and advanced integration mechanics allows engineers to design and build complex objects with rotational symmetry, such as aerodynamic jet engine turbines, rocket nozzles, and massive civil infrastructure like hyperbolic cooling towers. By analyzing these three-dimensional shapes as continuous cylindrical shells, aerospace and structural engineers can precisely calculate material weight, volume capacity, and fluid pressure distribution without having to invert mathematically complex boundary equations. Furthermore, these exact calculus principles are integrated into computer-aided manufacturing software to dictate the cutting paths of automated CNC lathes when machining curved automotive parts, ensuring structural integrity while optimizing material costs and reducing factory waste.

### Skill Block 1
Setting up a volume integral is only half the battle. Once your shell components are properly formulated using the core cylindrical shell theorems, you must execute the calculus. Because the shell method inherently multiplies a radius variable ($x$ or $y$) by a height expression, the resulting integrands frequently require specialized integration techniques centered around $u$-substitution and advanced exponent algebra.

## Explanatory Notes: Mastering the Substitution Methods

When evaluating a definite integral generated by the shell method without relying on integration by parts, you will typically depend on two main variation techniques of $u$-substitution:

### 1. Standard $u$-Substitution (The Radius as a Derivative Helper)

Many shell method problems feature highly complex internal composite functions. Normally, functions containing terms like $x^2$ inside a radical or exponential function are difficult to integrate on their own. However, because the shell method places a standalone variable ($x$) outside the function as the radius, it serves as the perfect "derivative helper" to complete a standard $u$-substitution.

### 2. Algebraic Back-Substitution (The "Change of Variables" Twist)

When the derivative of your chosen $u$ does not perfectly eliminate the remaining outer variables, you must use algebraic back-substitution. In this method, you explicitly solve your substitution equation for the original variable (e.g., rewriting $u = x - 1$ as $x = u + 1$) and substitute that expression back into the remaining outer terms of the integrand.

Whenever you apply either substitution method to a definite integral, always remember to convert your limits of integration to match your new variable $u$ before integrating.

## Sample Problems

### Sample Problem 1: Algebraic Back-Substitution ($u$-Substitution Twist)

**Problem:** Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x-1}$, the $x$-axis, and the vertical line $x = 5$ about the $y$-axis.

**Solution:**

1. **Setup:** Slicing parallel to the vertical $y$-axis gives a vertical shell layout ($dx$) evaluated over the interval from $x = 1$ to $x = 5$.
    
    - $\text{Radius } r(x) = x$
        
    - $\text{Height } h(x) = \sqrt{x-1} - 0 = \sqrt{x-1}$
        
        $$V = 2\pi \int_{1}^{5} x\sqrt{x-1} \, dx$$
        
2. **Algebraic Back-Substitution:** Let $u = x - 1 \implies du = dx$.
    
    - Notice that the outer radius variable $x$ does not naturally cancel out. We must solve for $x$ in terms of $u$:
        
        $$x = u + 1$$
        
    - Convert the integration limits to match $u$:
        
        - Lower limit: When $x = 1$, $u = 1 - 1 = 0$.
            
        - Upper limit: When $x = 5$, $u = 5 - 1 = 4$.
            
3. **Substitute and Distribute:** Replace all variables with $u$ terms and apply fractional exponents ($\sqrt{u} = u^{1/2}$):
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{4} (u + 1)u^{1/2} \, du$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{4} (u^{3/2} + u^{1/2}) \, du$$
    
4. **Integrate and Compute:** Apply the standard power rule to each term:
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{2}{5}u^{5/2} + \frac{2}{3}u^{3/2} \right]_{0}^{4}$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \left[ \frac{2}{5}(4)^{5/2} + \frac{2}{3}(4)^{3/2} \right] - 0 \right)$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{2}{5}(32) + \frac{2}{3}(8) \right) = 2\pi \left( \frac{64}{5} + \frac{16}{3} \right)$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{192 + 80}{15} \right) = \frac{544\pi}{15}$$
    

### Sample Problem 2: Standard $u$-Substitution (Rational Radical Integrand)

**Problem:** The region between the curve $y = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$ and the $x$-axis on the interval $[0,3]$ is rotated about the $y$-axis. Use the cylindrical shell method to find the exact volume of the generated solid.

**Solution:**

1. **Setup:** Slicing parallel to the vertical $y$-axis gives a standard $dx$ configuration over the interval $[0,3]$.
    
    - $\text{Radius } r(x) = x$
        
    - $\text{Height } h(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$
        
        $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{3} x \cdot \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}\right) \, dx = 2\pi \int_{0}^{3} \frac{x^2}{\sqrt{x^3+1}} \, dx$$
        
2. **Standard $u$-Substitution:** Let $u = x^3 + 1$. The derivative is $du = 3x^2 \, dx$, which means our outer $x^2$ term serves as the ideal derivative helper:
    
    $$x^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{3} \, du$$
    
    - Convert the integration limits to match $u$:
        
        - Lower limit: When $x = 0$, $u = 0^3 + 1 = 1$.
            
        - Upper limit: When $x = 3$, $u = 3^3 + 1 = 28$.
            
3. **Evaluate the Integral:**
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{1}^{28} \frac{1}{\sqrt{u}} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{3} \, du\right) = \frac{2\pi}{3} \int_{1}^{28} u^{-1/2} \, du$$
    
    $$V = \frac{2\pi}{3} \Big[ 2u^{1/2} \Big]_{1}^{28} = \frac{4\pi}{3} \Big[ \sqrt{u} \Big]_{1}^{28}$$
    
    $$V = \frac{4\pi}{3} (\sqrt{28} - \sqrt{1}) = \frac{4\pi}{3}(2\sqrt{7} - 1) = \frac{8\pi\sqrt{7} - 4\pi}{3}$$

##### Solution
###### Part 1: Finding the Volume Using the Shell Method

To find the volume of a solid generated by rotating a region around the $y$-axis using the **cylindrical shells method**, we imagine slicing the region into thin vertical strips. When rotated, each strip forms a cylindrical shell.

The standard formula for the volume using cylindrical shells is:

$$V = \int_{a}^{b} 2\pi \cdot (\text{radius}) \cdot (\text{height}) \, dx$$

###### Step 1: Identify the Components

- **Radius:** The distance from the axis of rotation ($y$-axis) to any vertical slice at position $x$. This is simply $\text{radius} = x$.

- **Height:** The top curve minus the bottom curve. The top curve is $y = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$ and the bottom boundary is the $x$-axis ($y = 0$). So, $\text{height} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$.

- **Limits of Integration:** The region spans along the $x$-axis from $x = 0$ to $x = 3$.


###### Step 2: Set Up the Integral

Substitute these components into our volume formula:

$$V = \int_{0}^{3} 2\pi \cdot x \cdot \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}\right) \, dx$$

Simplify the integrand by combining the $x$ terms in the numerator:

$$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{3} \frac{x^2}{\sqrt{x^3+1}} \, dx$$

###### Step 3: Evaluate Using $u$-Substitution

This integral is perfect for $u$-substitution because the numerator ($x^2$) is a scalar multiple of the derivative of the terms inside the square root ($x^3+1$).

1. Let $u = x^3 + 1$

2. Find the derivative: $du = 3x^2 \, dx \implies x^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{3} \, du$

3. Change the integration limits to match $u$:

- Lower limit: When $x = 0$, $u = 0^3 + 1 = 1$

- Upper limit: When $x = 3$, $u = 3^3 + 1 = 28$


Rewrite the integral in terms of $u$:

$$V = 2\pi \int_{1}^{28} \frac{1}{\sqrt{u}} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{3} \, du\right)$$

$$V = \frac{2\pi}{3} \int_{1}^{28} u^{-1/2} \, du$$

###### Step 4: Integrate and Compute

Using the power rule for integration ($\int u^n \, du = \frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1}$):

$$V = \frac{2\pi}{3} \left[ 2u^{1/2} \right]_{1}^{28}$$

$$V = \frac{4\pi}{3} \left[ \sqrt{u} \right]_{1}^{28}$$

Now apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$V = \frac{4\pi}{3} \left( \sqrt{28} - \sqrt{1} \right)$$

We can simplify $\sqrt{28}$ as $\sqrt{4 \times 7} = 2\sqrt{7}$:

$$V = \frac{4\pi}{3} (2\sqrt{7} - 1)$$

###### Final Exact Answer:

$$V = \frac{8\pi\sqrt{7} - 4\pi}{3} \text{ cubic units}$$

###### Part 2: Could you use Disks or Washers instead?

###### The Short Answer:

**Theoretically yes, but practically no.** ### The Detailed Explanation:

If you wanted to use the **washer method** to rotate this region around the $y$-axis, you would have to slice the region _horizontally_ rather than vertically. This introduces two massive complications:

1. **Inverting the Function:** Slicing horizontally means you must integrate with respect to $y$, which requires you to solve the original equation for $x$ in terms of $y$:

$$y = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}$$

If you square both sides and rearrange it, you get:

$$y^2(x^3 + 1) = x^2 \implies y^2x^3 - x^2 + y^2 = 0$$

This is a **cubic polynomial** in terms of $x$. While an analytical cubic formula exists, solving this for $x$ yields an extraordinarily messy, complex expression that is practically impossible to integrate by hand.

2. **Splitting the Region:** If you look closely at the peak of the curve in your graph, a horizontal line drawn across the top half of the shape would hit the curve twice (once on the way up, once on the way down). This means you would have to split your volume calculation into multiple separate integrals with different inner and outer boundaries.


**Conclusion:** The **shell method** is by far the superior choice here because it allows us to leave the function in terms of $x$, resulting in a clean, straightforward $u$-substitution.

To find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region around the $y$-axis, we use the cylindrical shell formula:

To verify the upper limit:

So, our limits are from $x = 1$ to $x = 4$.

Substitute these components into the shell formula:

$$V = \int_{1}^{4} 2\pi \cdot x \cdot (-\sqrt{x} + 2) \, dx$$

Pull the constant $2\pi$ out front and distribute the $x$ inside the integrand (remember that $x \cdot \sqrt{x} = x \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{3/2}$):

$$V = 2\pi \int_{1}^{4} (-x^{3/2} + 2x) \, dx$$

###### Step 3: Integrate Term by Term

Apply the power rule ($\int x^n \, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}$) to each term:

- For $-x^{3/2}$:

$$\int -x^{3/2} \, dx = -\frac{x^{5/2}}{5/2} = -\frac{2}{5}x^{5/2}$$

- For $2x$:

$$\int 2x \, dx = x^2$$


Now combine them into the antiderivative:

$$V = 2\pi \left[ -\frac{2}{5}x^{5/2} + x^2 \right]_{1}^{4}$$

###### Step 4: Evaluate the Limits

Now we calculate the values at the upper limit ($4$) and lower limit ($1$).

- **At $x = 4$:**

$$-\frac{2}{5}(4)^{5/2} + (4)^2 = - \frac{2}{5}(32) + 16 = -\frac{64}{5} + \frac{80}{5} = \frac{16}{5}$$

- **At $x = 1$:**

$$-\frac{2}{5}(1)^{5/2} + (1)^2 = -\frac{2}{5} + 1 = -\frac{2}{5} + \frac{5}{5} = \frac{3}{5}$$


Subtract the lower limit value from the upper limit value:

$$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{16}{5} - \frac{3}{5} \right) = 2\pi \left( \frac{13}{5} \right)$$

###### Final Exact Answer:

$$V = \frac{26\pi}{5} \text{ cubic units} \quad (\approx 16.34)$$

### Skill Block 2
Once you have identified the correct variable of integration ($dx$ vs. $dy$), the next critical skill is translating geometric distances into precise algebraic expressions. To compute the volume of a solid of revolution, you must accurately model the changing radius ($r$) and height ($h$) of the cylindrical shells, especially when the axis of rotation shifts away from the standard coordinate axes.

## Explanatory Notes: Building the Integrand

The cylindrical shell method is derived by treating a solid as an infinite sum of thin, nested cylinders. When one of these thin shells is cut vertically and unrolled flat, it forms a solid rectangular plate.

The volume of this unrolled plate is defined by the product of its geometric dimensions:

$$\text{Volume} = \text{Circumference} \times \text{Height} \times \text{Thickness}$$

Because the circumference of a circle is always $2\pi r$, the Riemann sum that defines this volume is written as:

$$\text{Volume} = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\pi \cdot r_i \cdot h_i \cdot \Delta x$$

In definite integral form, this yields our core formulation tool:

$$V = \int_{a}^{b} 2\pi \cdot [r(x)] \cdot [h(x)] \, dx \quad \text{or} \quad V = \int_{c}^{d} 2\pi \cdot [r(y)] \cdot [h(y)] \, dy$$

### 1. Formulating the Radius ($r$)

The radius represents the **perpendicular distance from the representative slicing strip to the axis of rotation**.

- **Standard Axes:** * If rotating about the $y$-axis, the radius is simply $x$.
    
    - If rotating about the $x$-axis, the radius is simply $y$.
        
- **Shifted Vertical Axes ($x = L$):** When rotating about a vertical line other than the $y$-axis, the radius must account for the spatial shift:
    
    - If the axis is to the left of the region ($L \le a$), the radius is $\mathbf{x - L}$.
        
    - If the axis is to the right of the region ($L \ge b$), the radius is $\mathbf{L - x}$.
        
- **Shifted Horizontal Axes ($y = L$):** When rotating about a horizontal line other than the $x$-axis:
    
    - If the axis is below the region ($L \le c$), the radius is $\mathbf{y - L}$.
        
    - If the axis is above the region ($L \ge d$), the radius is $\mathbf{L - y}$.
        

### 2. Formulating the Height ($h$)

The height represents the **total linear length of the representative slicing strip**.

- For vertical slices ($dx$ integrals), the height is the top boundary minus the bottom boundary: $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$.
    
- For horizontal slices ($dy$ integrals), the height is the right boundary minus the left boundary: $h(y) = f(y) - g(y)$.
    

## Sample Problems

### Sample Problem 1: Shifted Vertical Axis ($L \ge b$)

**Problem:** Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = x^2$, the $x$-axis ($y = 0$), and the vertical line $x = 2$ about the line $x = 4$.

**Solution:**

1. **Variable Selection:** Slicing parallel to the vertical axis $x = 4$ requires vertical slices, so we integrate with respect to $x$ ($dx$). The limits of integration span across the region from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$.
    
2. **Radius Formulation:** The axis of rotation ($x = 4$) lies to the **right** of our integration interval $[0, 2]$. Therefore, the distance from any slice at position $x$ to the line $x = 4$ is:
    
    $$r(x) = 4 - x$$
    
3. **Height Formulation:** The height of the vertical slice is bounded by the curve on top and the $x$-axis on the bottom:
    
    $$h(x) = x^2 - 0 = x^2$$
    
4. **Integration:**
    
    $$V = \int_{0}^{2} 2\pi (4 - x)(x^2) \, dx = 2\pi \int_{0}^{2} (4x^2 - x^3) \, dx$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{4x^3}{3} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{2} = 2\pi \left( \frac{32}{3} - 4 \right) = 2\pi \left( \frac{20}{3} \right) = \frac{40\pi}{3}$$
    

### Sample Problem 2: Region Bounded by Two Curves

**Problem:** Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = 2x$ and $y = x^2$ about the standard $y$-axis.

**Solution:**

1. **Variable Selection:** Slicing parallel to the $y$-axis requires vertical slices ($dx$). Setting the equations equal ($2x = x^2 \implies x(2-x) = 0$) gives the integration limits: $x = 0$ to $x = 2$.
    
2. **Radius Formulation:** Since we are rotating about the standard $y$-axis, the radius is simply:
    
    $$r(x) = x$$
    
3. **Height Formulation:** Over the interval $[0, 2]$, the linear line $y = 2x$ sits above the parabola $y = x^2$. The height of the shell is the difference between the two functions:
    
    $$h(x) = 2x - x^2$$
    
4. **Integration:**
    
    $$V = \int_{0}^{2} 2\pi \cdot x \cdot (2x - x^2) \, dx = 2\pi \int_{0}^{2} (2x^2 - x^3) \, dx$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{2x^3}{3} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{2} = 2\pi \left( \frac{16}{3} - 4 \right) = \frac{8\pi}{3}$$

$$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin(u) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2} \, du\right)$$

$$V = \pi \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \sin(u) \, du$$

The antiderivative of $\sin(u)$ is $-\cos(u)$:

$$V = \pi \Big[ -\cos(u) \Big]_{0}^{\pi/2}$$

$$V = \pi \left( -\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) - (-\cos(0)) \right)$$

Evaluate the trigonometric values:

- $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$

- $\cos(0) = 1$

$$V = \pi \left( 0 - (-1) \right) = \pi(1)$$

### Skill Block 3
The core of the cylindrical shell method relies on your ability to look at a 2D region, envision its 3D rotation, and choose the correct variable of integration ($dx$ vs. $dy$). When a region is difficult to rewrite in terms of the opposite variable, the disk or washer method becomes impractical, making the shell method an essential alternative.

## Explanatory Notes: The Core Mechanics

Unlike the disk and washer methods where slicing rectangles are drawn _perpendicular_ to the axis of rotation, the **cylindrical shell method requires slicing rectangles to be drawn _parallel_ to the axis of rotation**.

### 1. The Slicing Rules ($dx$ vs. $dy$)

- **Rotation around a Vertical Axis (like the $y$-axis or $x = L$):** Your thin representative slices must run vertically to stay parallel to the axis. Because the thickness of a vertical slice rests on the $x$-axis ($\Delta x$), you **must integrate with respect to $x$ ($dx$)**.
    
- **Rotation around a Horizontal Axis (like the $x$-axis or $y = L$):** Your thin representative slices must run horizontally to stay parallel to the axis. Because the thickness of a horizontal slice rests on the $y$-axis ($\Delta y$), you **must integrate with respect to $y$ ($dy$)**.
    

### 2. Identifying Boundaries (Height Formulation)

The height of your shell is determined by the length of your slice:

- For **$dx$ integrals**, the height is the upper boundary curve minus the lower boundary curve: $h(x) = f(x) - g(x)$.
    
- For **$dy$ integrals**, the height is the rightmost boundary curve minus the leftmost boundary curve: $h(y) = f(y) - g(y)$.
    

### 3. Finding Limits of Integration

The limits of integration span across the width of the original region, tracking the movement of the slices. If the boundary intersection points are not explicitly given, you must set the boundary equations equal to each other to solve for the intersection coordinates.

## Sample Problems

### Sample Problem 1: Vertical Axis Rotation ($dx$)

**Problem:** The region enclosed by the parabola $y = 4x - x^2$ and the $x$-axis ($y = 0$) is rotated about the $y$-axis. Set up and evaluate the integral for the volume of the solid.

**Solution:**

1. **Visualize and Select Variable:** The axis of rotation is vertical ($y$-axis). Slicing parallel means drawing vertical lines, so we integrate with respect to $x$ ($dx$).
    
2. **Find Limits:** Find where $y = 4x - x^2$ intersects the $x$-axis ($y = 0$):
    
    $$4x - x^2 = 0 \implies x(4 - x) = 0 \implies x = 0 \text{ and } x = 4$$
    
    Our limits are $a = 0$ and $b = 4$.
    
3. **Identify Shell Components:**
    
    - $\text{Radius } r(x) = x$ (distance from $y$-axis to slice)
        
    - $\text{Height } h(x) = (4x - x^2) - 0 = 4x - x^2$
        
4. **Set Up and Integrate:**
    
    $$V = \int_{0}^{4} 2\pi \cdot x \cdot (4x - x^2) \, dx$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{4} (4x^2 - x^3) \, dx$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{4x^3}{3} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{4}$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{4(64)}{3} - \frac{256}{4} \right) = 2\pi \left( \frac{256}{3} - 64 \right) = 2\pi \left( \frac{64}{3} \right) = \frac{128\pi}{3}$$
    

### Sample Problem 2: Horizontal Axis Rotation ($dy$)

**Problem:** The region bounded by $x = y^2$, the $y$-axis ($x = 0$), and the horizontal line $y = 2$ is rotated about the $x$-axis. Set up and evaluate the integral for the volume of the solid.

**Solution:**

1. **Visualize and Select Variable:** The axis of rotation is horizontal ($x$-axis). Slicing parallel means drawing horizontal lines, so we integrate with respect to $y$ ($dy$).
    
2. **Find Limits:** The region is bounded vertically between the $x$-axis ($y = 0$) and the line $y = 2$. Thus, our limits are $c = 0$ and $d = 2$.
    
3. **Identify Shell Components:**
    
    - $\text{Radius } r(y) = y$ (distance from $x$-axis to horizontal slice)
        
    - $\text{Height } h(y) = \text{Right} - \text{Left} = y^2 - 0 = y^2$
        
4. **Set Up and Integrate:**
    
    $$V = \int_{0}^{2} 2\pi \cdot y \cdot (y^2) \, dy$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{2} y^3 \, dy$$
    
    $$V = 2\pi \left[ \frac{y^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{2} = 2\pi \left( \frac{16}{4} - 0 \right) = 8\pi$$

#### Active Practice Problem
Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y=x^{2}$, $x=1$, and $y=0$ about the line $y=-1$.

![[07 - Volume by Shells LA Image 4.png]]

##### Workspace

##### Solution
Because this entire learning activity focuses on the **Shell Method**, we will solve it using shells first. However, since the axis of rotation here is **horizontal** ($y = -1$), using the shell method means our cylindrical shells will open horizontally, requiring us to integrate with respect to $y$ ($dy$).

###### Method 1: Finding the Volume Using the Shell Method ($dy$)

The formula for horizontal shells rotated around a horizontal axis is:

$$V = \int_{c}^{d} 2\pi \cdot (\text{radius}) \cdot (\text{height}) \, dy$$

###### Step 1: Identify the Components

- **Radius:** The vertical distance from the axis of rotation ($y = -1$) up to a horizontal slice at height $y$.

$$\text{radius} = \text{top} - \text{bottom} = y - (-1) = y + 1$$

- **Height:** The length of the horizontal slice, which is the right-hand boundary minus the left-hand boundary.

- Right boundary: $x = 1$

- Left boundary: $y = x^2 \implies x = \sqrt{y}$

$$\text{height} = 1 - \sqrt{y}$$

- **Limits of Integration ($c$ to $d$):** We track the region along the $y$-axis. It starts at the bottom ($y = 0$) and goes up to where the curve meets the line $x = 1$, which is $y = (1)^2 = 1$. So, our limits are from $y = 0$ to $y = 1$.


###### Step 2: Set Up and Expand the Integral

Substitute the components into the shell formula:

$$V = \int_{0}^{1} 2\pi \cdot (y + 1) \cdot (1 - \sqrt{y}) \, dy$$

Pull $2\pi$ out front and foil/expand the binomials inside (using $y^{1/2}$ for $\sqrt{y}$):

$$(y + 1)(1 - y^{1/2}) = y - y^{3/2} + 1 - y^{1/2}$$

Rearranging the terms nicely:

$$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{1} \left( 1 + y - y^{1/2} - y^{3/2} \right) \, dy$$

###### Step 3: Integrate Term by Term

Apply the power rule to each component:

$$V = 2\pi \left[ y + \frac{y^2}{2} - \frac{2}{3}y^{3/2} - \frac{2}{5}y^{5/2} \right]_{0}^{1}$$

###### Step 4: Evaluate the Limits

Since plugging in the lower limit ($y = 0$) yields $0$, we only need to evaluate the upper limit ($y = 1$):

$$V = 2\pi \left( 1 + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{3} - \frac{2}{5} \right)$$

Find a common denominator for the fractions inside the parentheses (which is 30):

$$1 + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{3} - \frac{2}{5} = \frac{30}{30} + \frac{15}{30} - \frac{20}{30} - \frac{12}{30} = \frac{13}{30}$$

Now, multiply by the $2\pi$ outside:

$$V = 2\pi \left( \frac{13}{30} \right) = \frac{26\pi}{30}$$

###### Final Exact Answer:

$$V = \frac{13\pi}{15} \text{ cubic units} \quad (\approx 2.72)$$

###### Method 2: An Easier Alternative (The Washer Method)

While the packet is meant for practice with shells, this specific geometry is actually much faster to solve using the **Washer Method** ($dx$). Since the rotation is around a horizontal axis, the washer method allows us to stay in terms of $x$, saving us from having to change the original equations into square roots.

###### The Washer Setup:

- **Outer Radius ($R(x)$):** The distance from $y = -1$ up to the top curve $y = x^2$.

$$R(x) = x^2 - (-1) = x^2 + 1$$

- **Inner Radius ($r(x)$):** The distance from $y = -1$ up to the flat bottom boundary $y = 0$.

$$r(x) = 0 - (-1) = 1$$

- **Limits:** Along the $x$-axis from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$.


$$V = \int_{0}^{1} \pi \left[ [R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2 \right] \, dx$$

$$V = \pi \int_{0}^{1} \left[ (x^2 + 1)^2 - (1)^2 \right] \, dx$$

$$V = \pi \int_{0}^{1} \left( x^4 + 2x^2 + 1 - 1 \right) \, dx$$

$$V = \pi \int_{0}^{1} \left( x^4 + 2x^2 \right) \, dx$$

Integrating this gives:

$$V = \pi \left[ \frac{x^5}{5} + \frac{2x^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{1} = \pi \left( \frac{1}{5} + \frac{2}{3} \right) = \pi \left( \frac{3+10}{15} \right) = \frac{13\pi}{15}$$

Both methods yield the exact same elegant answer!
